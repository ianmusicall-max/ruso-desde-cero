> **Today** you discover that every Russian has three names, why the neighbour in the entrance hall is called **Ве́ра Ива́новна**, and what to do with a Russian form. By the end you'll know how to fill it in.

## 1. Before you read (0–5 min)

In this course's story there's a neighbour everyone calls **Ве́ра
Ива́новна**. Ве́ра is her first name. And **Ива́новна**? Before you read,
note down what you think it is: a middle name, a surname, something else?

A clue in English: **Johnson** once meant "son of John", and **Robertson**,
"son of Robert". Today they're surnames and nobody thinks of the father. In
Russian, that idea is still alive.

## 2. Text (5–18 min)

### Three names in one

Every Russian has three names: a **surname** (**фами́лия**), a **first name**
(**и́мя**) and a **patronymic** (**о́тчество**), which comes from the father's
first name. **Ве́ра Ива́новна Петро́ва** is called Vera, her father was called
Ivan and her surname is Petrova.

### How the patronymic is made

You take the father's first name and add an ending: `-ович` for a son and
`-овна` for a daughter. If the father's name ends in **й**, the o becomes e:
`-евич`, `-евна`.

| The father | The son | The daughter |
|---|---|---|
| Ива́н | Ива́нович | Ива́новна |
| Анто́н | Анто́нович | Анто́новна |
| Макси́м | Макси́мович | Макси́мовна |
| Серге́й | Серге́евич | Серге́евна |
| Андре́й | Андре́евич | Андре́евна |

The stress stays where it was in the father's name.

### When it's used

First name plus patronymic is the respectful form: it goes with **вы**. It's
how you address your teacher, the doctor, the older neighbour or your boss:
**Ве́ра Ива́новна, здра́вствуйте!** Where English would say "Mrs Petrova",
Russian says **Ве́ра Ива́новна**. With friends, family and colleagues, the
first name is enough, and nearly always in its short form: **Ма́ша**,
**Са́ша**.

### The surname is his or hers too

Russian surnames change like nationalities: **Ивано́в** is him and
**Ивано́ва** is her; **Петро́в**, him, and **Петро́ва**, her. In one family,
the father is **Анто́н Петро́в** and the daughter, **Ни́на Петро́ва**.

### The form

On Russian forms and documents, the order is fixed: surname first, then first
name, then patronymic. It even has an abbreviation, **ФИО** [fee-O]:
**фами́лия, и́мя, о́тчество**. You'll see it on every piece of paper, from
the migration card to a hotel registration form.

Nearly all foreigners have no patronymic: that box is left blank. And a
middle name isn't a patronymic: copy your name exactly as it is in your
passport.

## 3. Words (18–23 min)

| Russian | English | Example |
|---|---|---|
| о́тчество | patronymic | Ива́новна — э́то о́тчество. |
| Как вас по о́тчеству? | What's your patronymic? | Ве́ра… А как вас по о́тчеству? |
| ФИО | full name (surname, first name, patronymic) | ФИО: Петро́ва Ве́ра Ива́новна. |
| па́спорт | passport | Вот па́спорт. |
| гражда́нство | citizenship | Гражда́нство: Кана́да. |
| докуме́нт | document | Вот па́спорт и докуме́нт. |
| анке́та | form, questionnaire | Вот анке́та. ФИО? |

**Как вас по о́тчеству?** is what someone asks when they want to address you
respectfully: a Russian will ask you soon, and now you know to answer that
you don't have one.

## 4. Task (23–29 min)

### А. Ве́рно и́ли неве́рно?

*True or false?*

1. The patronymic comes from the father's first name.
2. You call your teacher by her surname: "Петро́ва!".
3. A woman in the Ivanov family has the surname **Ивано́ва**.
4. On a Russian form, the surname comes first.
5. Friends call each other by their patronymics.

### Б. Как его́ о́тчество?

*This is the father's name. What's the son's patronymic, and the daughter's?*

1. Анто́н
2. Макси́м
3. Серге́й

### В. Анке́та.

> Фами́лия: …

## 5. Wrap-up (29–30 min)
