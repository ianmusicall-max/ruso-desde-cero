> **Today** you find out why Russians ask what you do five minutes after meeting you, and you learn to tell apart three words that English rolls into one or two.

## 1. Before you read (0–5 min)

Think for a moment before reading: in your country, when do people ask what
someone does for a living? In the first conversation, or later? Who would
you never ask?

Write your answer in one line. At the end of the text you'll see whether it
matches.

## 2. Text (5–18 min)

### Three words and a little book

In a Russian conversation, **Кем вы рабо́таете?** comes early. It can turn up
in the first quarter of an hour, somewhere between the name and the city,
and it doesn't sound nosy: it's a way of placing the person, like asking
where they're from. To someone arriving from Britain or North America it can
feel blunt; to the person asking it is the most natural thing in the world.

And where English manages with "work" and "job", Russian uses three words
that don't mean the same thing:

- **рабо́та** is the work you do now — the place and the hours.
- **профе́ссия** is the trade, what you are even if you aren't doing it
  today.
- **специа́льность** is what you trained for, what your diploma says.

All three can fail to match, and that's normal: someone whose
**специа́льность** is engineering may have a **рабо́та** driving a taxi.
That's why there are two questions: **Кем вы рабо́таете?** asks about the
first, and **Кто вы по профе́ссии?** about the second.

There's also an object that surprises foreigners: the **трудова́я кни́жка**,
a personal booklet in which each job was written down with its dates, stamp
after stamp. For decades it was the piece of paper that proved your whole
working life. That record is now going electronic [VERIFICAR: from which
year and on what terms], but the name is still alive in the language and in
people's heads.

One last thing worth knowing: asking about **pay** is not part of this. The
question about the job is social; the question about money, in Russia as
nearly everywhere, isn't asked.

## 3. Words (18–23 min)

| Russian | English | Example |
|---|---|---|
| специа́льность | field of study, qualification | Моя́ специа́льность — инжене́р. |
| трудова́я кни́жка | employment record book | Вот трудова́я кни́жка. |
| Кто вы по профе́ссии? | What's your profession? | — Кто вы по профе́ссии? — Я врач. |
| зарпла́та | salary, pay | Зарпла́та — не вопро́с. |
| нача́льник | boss | Э́то мой нача́льник. |
| колле́га | colleague | Э́то мой колле́га. |

**Кто вы по профе́ссии?** comes in whole, like **Кем вы рабо́таете?**: why
the words change is A2 material.

## 4. Task (23–29 min)

### А. Ве́рно и́ли неве́рно?

*True or false?*

1. In Russia people ask about your job soon after meeting you.
2. **Рабо́та** and **профе́ссия** mean the same thing.
3. **Специа́льность** is what your diploma says.
4. The **трудова́я кни́жка** is a booklet with your jobs written in it.
5. Asking about pay is as ordinary as asking about the job.

### Б. Рабо́та, профе́ссия и́ли специа́льность?

*Which word would you use in each case?*

1. You studied medicine and you have the degree.
2. Today you teach English at a school.
3. You're a cook, even if you aren't cooking this month.

### В. Вы и ва́ша рабо́та.

*Write three sentences about yourself with what you know:*

> Я… Моя́ специа́льность — … Я рабо́таю.

## 5. Wrap-up (29–30 min)

> **You now know:** that **Кем вы рабо́таете?** comes early and isn't nosy; that **рабо́та**, **профе́ссия** and **специа́льность** are three different things; and that pay isn't discussed.
