> **Today** you go into the Moscow metro: why its stations look like museums, which words you'll see written up, and why **ста́нция** and **вокза́л** aren't the same thing. By the end you'll know how to get around inside.

## 1. Before you read (0–5 min)

You already know two words that look like the same one and aren't:

> вокза́л (railway station, from day 7 of week 3) · ста́нция (metro station)

Before you read, think: in your city, how do you know which way a metro
train is going? Note in one line where you look. At the end of the text
you'll see what a Russian does.

## 2. Text (5–18 min)

### A palace underground

The first Moscow metro line opened in 1935. The stations of those years were
built like ballrooms: marble, chandeliers, mosaics, stained glass and
statues. The idea was that everyday transport should be a beautiful place,
and the result is that many of those stations are now protected as heritage
and turn up in the guidebooks.

Some stations are very deep —the ones that doubled as shelters— and getting
down to them means enormously long escalators. You stand on the right and
leave the left clear for people in a hurry, which is exactly the opposite of
where people stand when they don't know.

### The words you'll see

| Russian | English |
|---|---|
| вход | entrance |
| вы́ход | exit |
| вы́ход в го́род | way out to the street |
| переса́дка | interchange |
| пе́реход | the walkway between lines |
| осторо́жно | careful, mind out |

**Вход** and **вы́ход** look very alike and mean the opposite: the
difference is the stress and that **вы-** at the front. Look at them twice.

### How you pay

You pay with a card. The Moscow one is called **Тро́йка**, you top it up at
the machines and the ticket windows, and it works on the bus and the tram
too. In St Petersburg the card is a different one, the **Подоро́жник**.
Fares and payment methods change every year: **[VERIFICAR] before quoting a
price**.

### And the answer to the question at the start

In the Moscow metro, the direction isn't on a sign: **you hear it**. On
trains heading into the centre, a man's voice makes the announcements; on
trains heading out, a woman's. On the circle line it's the other way round:
a man's voice clockwise, a woman's anticlockwise. It was designed to help
people who can't see well, and everybody uses it.

## 4. Task (23–29 min)

### А. Ве́рно и́ли неве́рно?

*True or false?*

1. The Moscow metro opened in 1935.
2. On the escalators you leave the right-hand side clear.
3. **Ста́нция** and **вокза́л** mean the same thing.
4. In Moscow you pay with a card called **Тро́йка**.
5. The voice announcing the stops tells you which way the train is going.

### Б. Вход и́ли вы́ход?

*Which sign are you looking for?*

1. You've just arrived and want to get into the metro.
2. You've reached your stop and want to get out to the street.
3. You need to change lines.

### В. Ваш го́род.

*Write three sentences about transport in your city, with what you know:*

> В це́нтре — метро́. Вот ста́нция. Метро́ далеко́ и́ли ря́дом?

## 5. Wrap-up (29–30 min)

> **Now you know:** a **ста́нция** is a metro station and a **вокза́л** a railway one; on the escalator you keep the left clear; and in Moscow the direction of the train is in the voice.

## Answers

**А.** 1 true · 2 false: you leave the left clear · 3 false: **ста́нция** is
metro and **вокза́л** is rail · 4 true · 5 true: a man's or a woman's voice,
depending on the direction

**Б.** 1 вход · 2 вы́ход (or вы́ход в го́род) · 3 переса́дка

**В.** For example: **Э́то мой го́род. В це́нтре — метро́. Ста́нция ря́дом.**
