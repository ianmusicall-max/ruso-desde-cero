Where the Cyrillic alphabet comes from

Отку́да кири́ллица

Draft: this lesson hasn't had its final Russian review yet. It may change.

Today you find out why seven letters fooled you this week, who invented the Russian alphabet (not the man it's named after) and how Russian names get shortened. By the end you'll be able to write your name in Cyrillic.

1Before you read0–5 min

On day 2 four letters fooled you, and on day 3 three more:

С Р У Х В Е Н

Before you read, think: why do they look so much like ours and sound different? Jot your idea down in one line. At the end of the text you'll see if you were right.

A clue. This word is Greek, ΧΡΟΝΟΣ: it means "time", and it's where chronology comes from. Read the Χ like the ch in loch and the Ρ like an r: [khro-nos]. Do those two letters ring a bell?

2Text5–18 min

Two brothers and an alphabet

In the year 863, two brothers born in Thessalonica set off north from Byzantium: Constantine and Methodius. The prince of Great Moravia, a Slavic kingdom in central Europe, had asked for teachers who would preach to his people in their own language. But the Slavs had no writing.

Constantine invented it. He created a brand-new alphabet, with its own letter shapes, designed for Slavic sounds: Glagolitic (глаго́лица). With it the brothers translated the texts they needed. Constantine died in Rome in 869, after becoming a monk under the name Cyril; Methodius carried on the work until his death in 885.

The alphabet that bears his name

The alphabet you're learning isn't that one. Towards the end of the 9th century, in Bulgaria, the brothers' disciples made another one, easier for anyone who already knew Greek: they took the Greek letters and added the ones Slavic sounds needed. It's called Cyrillic (кири́ллица) in Cyril's honour, though he never saw it.

And here's the answer to the question at the start. The letters that fool you aren't misread English letters: they're Greek, and they sound the way they sounded in Greek. С is sigma, which was written round; Р is rho, already an r; Х is chi, a throaty sound like the ch in loch; and В is beta, which by then the Greeks already pronounced [v]. Our alphabet comes from Greek too, by another road: Latin and Cyrillic are cousins.

The final touch

In 1708 Tsar Peter I ordered the shapes of the letters to be simplified for printed books. The new type, the civil script (гражда́нский шрифт), looked more like European printing type, and that's where the shapes you read today come from.

Russian today uses 33 letters. Cyrillic is shared by Ukrainian, Belarusian, Bulgarian, Serbian and Macedonian, and by languages in Asia such as Kazakh, Kyrgyz, Tajik and Mongolian; each one has adjusted it to its own sounds, with a letter or two of its own. Every 24 May, Russia and Bulgaria celebrate the day of Slavic writing in honour of the two brothers.

Names that shrink

In Russian almost every name has a short form for family and friends, like Bill for William or Peggy for Margaret. And the short form can look very little like the long one:

Name Short form
А́нна А́ня
Мари́я Ма́ша
Алекса́ндр Са́ша
Ива́н Ва́ня
Дми́трий Ди́ма
Екатери́на Ка́тя
Михаи́л Ми́ша

Са́ша can be Alexander or Alexandra. The short form with someone you don't know sounds too familiar; the long one with a friend sounds serious, almost cross.

А. Ве́рно и́ли неве́рно?

True or false?

  1. Cyril invented the alphabet Russian uses today.
  2. The letters that fool you are Greek letters with their Greek sound.
  3. The Latin and the Cyrillic alphabets both come from Greek.
  4. In Russian, the short form of a name is used with everyone.
  5. Са́ша can be a man or a woman.
