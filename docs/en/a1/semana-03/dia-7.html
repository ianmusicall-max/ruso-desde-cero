Words that travelled

Слова́-путеше́ственники

Draft: this lesson hasn't had its final Russian review yet. It may change.

Today you find out where Russian got some of its words, why a railway station is named after some London gardens, and which three Russian words are going to fool you. By the end you'll know which box on a Russian form is for your surname.

1Before you read0–5 min

Four Russian words. You can read them now: say them aloud.

вокза́л · пальто́ · бутербро́д · футбо́л

One comes from English, one from French and one from German. And one of them means "railway station". Before you read, note down which one you think it is.

Think of English, too: "ballet" comes from French, "kindergarten" from German, "tomato" from Nahuatl by way of Spanish. Words travel with things.

2Text5–18 min

Words with passports

No language lives alone. When people bring something in from outside —an invention, a fashion, a dish— they often bring the word along too.

Russian has always done it. Greek words came with religion and the alphabet. In the time of Peter I, who wanted to build a fleet, ship words came in from Dutch and German. In the 18th and 19th centuries, when the Russian nobility spoke French in their salons, hundreds of French words came in. And in the 20th century came the English ones: sport first, technology later.

The station that was a garden

London had some famous pleasure gardens at Vauxhall, with music, lights and walks. In Russia, their name came to be used for places of entertainment with music.

In 1837 Russia's first railway opened, running out of St Petersburg, and soon the line reached Pavlovsk. There, at the end of the tracks, stood a building with a concert hall: the вокза́л of Pavlovsk. People took the train to go and hear music, and for several summers the Viennese Johann Strauss the Younger conducted the orchestra there.

In time, the word stuck to the building where the trains arrive. Today a вокза́л is a city's railway station. Careful, though: a metro station isn't a вокза́л, it's a ста́нция. And Vauxhall in London has a railway station of its own today, so the word has, in a way, come home.

What changed on the way

A word that travels doesn't always arrive the same:

  • пальто́ comes from the French paletot, a short outer garment. In Russian it's the long, warm winter coat.
  • бутербро́д comes from the German Butterbrot, "bread and butter". In Russian it's any slice of bread with something on top: butter, cheese, sausage, fish. And look at the end: the д sounds [t]. You know why.
  • футбо́л came from English and only changed its stress: "FOOTball" leans on the first syllable and футбо́л on the last.

False friends

Some words look like one of ours and mean something else. They're false friends, and they fool you precisely because you think you understand them:

Word It's not… It's…
фами́лия family (that's семья́) surname
магази́н a magazine (that's журна́л) a shop
аккура́тный accurate neat, careful

On a Russian form, the фами́лия box is for your surname; your first name goes in и́мя. And if someone calls you аккура́тный, it's a compliment: you're the kind who folds their clothes and turns up on time.
