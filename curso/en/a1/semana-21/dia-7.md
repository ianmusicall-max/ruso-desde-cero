> **Today there are no new words: there's a known word with a different meaning.** You read a scene at a hotel's front desk and check how much you understand without a dictionary.

## 1. Before you read (0–5 min)

**The same Russian word can mean two different things, depending on
where you hear it.** Before reading, think: in English, "number"
also has more than one sense —a phone number, or a house number—.
Can you think of another example?

## 2. Text (5–18 min)

### В гости́нице

*Someone arrives at a hotel's front desk.*

> — Здра́вствуйте! Где мой но́мер?
> — Ваш но́мер? Два́дцать четы́ре.
> — Два́дцать четы́ре? Спаси́бо. А где ключ?
> — Вот ключ. Он на столе́.
> — А где телефо́н?
> — Телефо́н то́же на столе́.

## 3. Words (18–23 min)

**You already knew но́мер meaning "number" (a phone number); at a
hotel it means "room."** It's the same word, a new sense understood
from context. **Два́дцать четы́ре** isn't new either: it's two
numbers you already knew, side by side, just like "twenty" and
"four" form "twenty-four" in English.

| Russian | English | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Где мой но́мер? | Where is my room? | Где мой но́мер? |

## 4. Task (23–29 min)

### А. Ве́рно и́ли неве́рно?

*True or false?*

1. The room is number 42.
2. The keys are on the table.
3. The phone is on the table.

### Б. Переведи́те.

*Translate into English.*

1. Ваш но́мер? Два́дцать четы́ре.
2. Телефо́н то́же на столе́.

### В. Same word, two meanings

*Think of another Russian word you already know that, in a different context, could mean something else. If none comes to mind, explain how you decided that "но́мер" meant "room" and not "phone number" in this text.*

## 5. Wrap-up (29–30 min)

> **Now you know:** that a familiar word can change meaning depending on context, with no new word needed to understand it.
>
> With this you close unit 11's first week.

## Answers

**А.** 1 false: it's 24 · 2 true · 3 true

**Б.** 1 Your room? Twenty-four. · 2 The phone is also on the table.
