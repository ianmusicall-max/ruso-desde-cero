> **Today** you find out where Russian got some of its words, why a railway station is named after some London gardens, and which three Russian words are going to fool you. By the end you'll know which box on a Russian form is for your surname.

## 1. Before you read (0–5 min)

Four Russian words. You can read them now: say them aloud.

> вокза́л · пальто́ · бутербро́д · футбо́л

One comes from English, one from French and one from German. And one of them
means "railway station". Before you read, note down which one you think it
is.

Think of English, too: "ballet" comes from French, "kindergarten" from
German, "tomato" from Nahuatl by way of Spanish. Words travel with things.

## 2. Text (5–18 min)

### Words with passports

No language lives alone. When people bring something in from outside —an
invention, a fashion, a dish— they often bring the word along too.

Russian has always done it. Greek words came with religion and the alphabet.
In the time of Peter I, who wanted to build a fleet, ship words came in from
Dutch and German. In the 18th and 19th centuries, when the Russian nobility
spoke French in their salons, hundreds of French words came in. And in the
20th century came the English ones: sport first, technology later.

### The station that was a garden

London had some famous pleasure gardens at Vauxhall, with music, lights and
walks. In Russia, their name came to be used for places of entertainment with
music.

In 1837 Russia's first railway opened, running out of St Petersburg, and soon
the line reached Pavlovsk. There, at the end of the tracks, stood a building
with a concert hall: the **вокза́л** of Pavlovsk. People took the train to go
and hear music, and for several summers the Viennese Johann Strauss the
Younger conducted the orchestra there.

In time, the word stuck to the building where the trains arrive. Today a
**вокза́л** is a city's railway station. Careful, though: a metro station
isn't a **вокза́л**, it's a **ста́нция**. And Vauxhall in London has a
railway station of its own today, so the word has, in a way, come home.

### What changed on the way

A word that travels doesn't always arrive the same:

- **пальто́** comes from the French *paletot*, a short outer garment. In
  Russian it's the long, warm winter coat.
- **бутербро́д** comes from the German *Butterbrot*, "bread and butter". In
  Russian it's any slice of bread with something on top: butter, cheese,
  sausage, fish. And look at the end: the д sounds [t]. You know why.
- **футбо́л** came from English and only changed its stress: "FOOTball"
  leans on the first syllable and **футбо́л** on the last.

### False friends

Some words look like one of ours and mean something else. They're **false
friends**, and they fool you precisely because you think you understand
them:

| Word | It's not… | It's… |
|---|---|---|
| фами́лия | family (that's **семья́**) | surname |
| магази́н | a magazine (that's **журна́л**) | a shop |
| аккура́тный | accurate | neat, careful |

On a Russian form, the **фами́лия** box is for your surname; your first name
goes in **и́мя**. And if someone calls you **аккура́тный**, it's a compliment:
you're the kind who folds their clothes and turns up on time.

## 4. Task (23–29 min)

### А. Ве́рно и́ли неве́рно?

*True or false?*

1. **Вокза́л** is named after some gardens in London.
2. In Russian, **фами́лия** means family.
3. To buy bread, you go to the **магази́н**.
4. A metro station is a **вокза́л**.
5. A Russian **бутербро́д** always has butter on it.

### Б. Как по-ру́сски?

*How do you say it in Russian?*

1. surname
2. shop
3. coat
4. railway station
5. neat, careful

а) магази́н · б) вокза́л · в) аккура́тный · г) фами́лия · д) пальто́

### В. Your form

Write by hand, in Cyrillic, the first two boxes of a Russian form with your
details, the way they sound:

> Фами́лия: …
> И́мя: …

## 5. Wrap-up (29–30 min)

> **Now you know:** a **вокза́л** was a garden with music, a Russian **бутербро́д** can have anything on top, and **фами́лия**, **магази́н** and **аккура́тный** aren't what they seem.

## Answers

**Before you read.** **Вокза́л** is the station. **Футбо́л** comes from
English, **пальто́** from French and **бутербро́д** from German.

**А.** 1 true · 2 false: it's the surname; family is **семья́** · 3 true · 4
false: it's a **ста́нция** · 5 false: it can have anything on top

**Б.** 1 г · 2 а · 3 д · 4 б · 5 в

**В.** For example: **Фами́лия: Смит. И́мя: Джон.** The surname goes first,
as on almost every Russian form.
